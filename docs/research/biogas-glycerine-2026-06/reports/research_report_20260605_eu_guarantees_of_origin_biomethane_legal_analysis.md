## Executive Summary

EU law clearly permits biomethane Guarantees of Origin (GOs) to be transferred independently of the physical energy for disclosure purposes. Recital 53 of the Renewable Energy Directive II (Directive 2018/2001) states that a GO can be transferred independently of the energy to which it relates, and Article 19 allows transfers separately from physical energy [1][2]. That rule must be distinguished from Proofs of Sustainability (PoS), which support compliance with sustainability and greenhouse-gas criteria under Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/996. PoS operate through a mass-balance chain, not as an unrestricted book-and-claim certificate.

The EU interconnected gas grid is treated as one mass-balancing system, and the definition of interconnected infrastructure includes pipelines, LNG terminals and storage facilities [3]. That can allow sustainability characteristics to be reassigned within a qualifying integrated infrastructure, but it does not by itself establish that any fossil LNG purchase may be labelled compliant bio-LNG. The Commission's current UDB documentation describes verified injection volumes, Transfer Gas PoS movements within the integrated grid, and registration at the exit or final-consumption point [26]. The current Dutch draft implementing order likewise requires the same mass-balance-traceable delivery to carry both a GO and a PoS [28]. The strongest defensible conclusion is therefore conditional: GOs can be unbundled for disclosure, while PoS-based claims remain subject to mass-balance, UDB, voluntary-scheme and national compliance rules. The documents reviewed here show an active dispute over those boundaries, not a settled EU-law endorsement of unrestricted fossil-LNG relabelling.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Scope and Methodology

This report identifies and analyses the EU provisions that separate disclosure GOs from compliance PoS for biomethane, and assesses what the mass-balance rules do and do not establish for gas and LNG. It covers primary legislation (RED II, RED III, Gas and Hydrogen Package), implementing regulations (IR 2022/996), technical standards (CEN EN 16325), institutional positions (ENTSOG, ERGaR, AIB), court decisions (CJEU), and critical academic and legal analysis. All legal citations reference specific articles, recitals, or paragraphs with verbatim or near-verbatim language drawn from official sources.

### 1.2 Why This Matters

The GO system was originally designed for electricity and has been extended to gas. Its extension to gas -- and specifically to the LNG supply chain -- raises distinct questions because GOs serve disclosure while PoS serve sustainability compliance through mass balance. The distinction has consequences for corporate emissions reporting, EU ETS compliance, national blending mandates, and public trust in "green gas" claims.

## 2. Article 19 of RED II (Directive 2018/2001): The Foundational Provision

### 2.1 The Core Decoupling Principle

Article 19 of Directive (EU) 2018/2001, titled "Guarantees of origin for energy from renewable sources," establishes the legal architecture for GOs across all energy carriers. The provision most relevant to the decoupling question appears in multiple formulations.

**Recital 53** of RED II provides the legislative intent: "Guarantees of origin issued for the purposes of this Directive have the sole function of showing to a final customer that a given share or quantity of energy was produced from renewable sources. A guarantee of origin can be transferred, independently of the energy to which it relates, from one holder to another" [1]. The recital continues: "energy from renewable sources in relation to which the accompanying guarantee of origin has been sold separately by the producer should not be disclosed or sold to the final customer as energy from renewable sources" [1]. This anti-double-counting clause means that once a producer sells the GO separately, the physical energy loses its "renewable" label -- and conversely, the GO holder gains the right to label their consumption as renewable regardless of what physical molecules they consume.

**Article 19(2)** operationalises this by establishing the issuance framework. Member States "shall ensure that a guarantee of origin is issued in response to a request from a producer of energy from renewable sources" and that each GO is "of the standard size of 1 MWh" [2]. Critically, the article provides that "Transfers of guarantees of origin, separately or together with the physical transfer of energy, shall have no effect on the decision of Member States to use statistical transfers, joint projects or joint support schemes for compliance with Article 3" [2]. This language -- "separately or together with" -- codifies the legal permissibility of unbundled GO trading.

### 2.2 Definition and Legal Status

Article 2(12) of RED II defines a guarantee of origin as "an electronic document which has the sole function of providing evidence to a final customer that a given share or quantity of energy was produced from renewable sources" [6]. The word "evidence" replaced "proof" from the predecessor RED I (Directive 2009/28/EC, Article 15), a deliberate linguistic softening that scholars have noted reduces the epistemic burden the instrument must carry [7].

### 2.3 Scope Extension to Gas

**Recital 59** of RED II explicitly extends the GO system beyond electricity: "Guarantees of origin which are currently in place for renewable electricity should be extended to cover renewable gas" [1]. Article 19(7) implements this by requiring that GO specifications conform to the CEN EN 16325 standard, which since its 2020 revision has been expanded to cover "electricity, gaseous hydrocarbons, Hydrogen, and heating & cooling" [8].

### 2.4 Validity and Cancellation

Article 19(3) establishes that GOs expire 18 months after the energy unit is produced, with the standard validity period being 12 months [2]. Article 19(4) requires energy companies to cancel GOs within six months after expiry [2]. This temporal window creates a market for GO trading during the validity period.

## 3. The Gas and Hydrogen Package (2024): Extending the Framework

### 3.1 Directive 2024/1788

Directive (EU) 2024/1788 of 13 June 2024, on common rules for the internal markets for renewable gas, natural gas and hydrogen, entered into force on 4 August 2024 with a transposition deadline of 5 August 2026 [4]. The directive extends the GO framework to gas markets with several key provisions.

The directive provides that "where guarantees of origin have been issued for the production of a consignment of low-carbon gas, they shall be subject to the same rules as those set out in that Article for guarantees of origin issued for the production of renewable gas" [4]. This means the decoupling principle established under Article 19 of RED II applies with equal force to low-carbon gas certificates -- extending the book-and-claim logic to a broader category of fuels.

The directive also requires Member States to require relevant operators to enter transactions and sustainability characteristics into the Union Database or linked national databases [4]. The Commission's current UDB documentation says the database covers gaseous renewable and recycled-carbon fuels, including biomethane; injected volumes are verified by TSOs/DSOs, Transfer Gas PoS can move within the integrated grid, and a formal transaction is recorded at exit or final consumption [26]. The UDB provides volume and certificate traceability, but it is not molecule-level physical tracing and does not eliminate the need to test the applicable national compliance rules.

### 3.2 Regulation 2024/1789

Regulation (EU) 2024/1789 on the internal markets for renewable gas, natural gas and hydrogen has been directly applicable in Member States since 5 February 2025 [9]. The regulation supports the GO framework by establishing infrastructure access rights, requiring that Member States "enable the access of renewable and low-carbon gases to the market and infrastructure regardless of whether the production facilities are connected to distribution or transmission networks" [9]. It also provides for tariff discounts of up to 100% for renewable gas and 75% for low-carbon gas at entry points and storage facilities [9].

### 3.3 Article 9: Low-Carbon Fuels Methodology

Article 9 of Directive 2024/1788 mandates the Commission to adopt a delegated act specifying "the methodology for assessing greenhouse gas emissions savings from low-carbon fuels," consistent with the methodology for RFNBOs (renewable fuels of non-biological origin) and RCFs (recycled carbon fuels) under RED II [4]. The Commission may also adopt decisions recognising voluntary schemes for low-carbon fuel certification, provided they meet "adequate standards of reliability, transparency and independent auditing in line with the requirements set out in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/996" [4].

## 4. RED III (Directive 2023/2413): Strengthening and Amending Article 19

Directive (EU) 2023/2413 of 18 October 2023 (RED III) amended several provisions of Article 19. Key changes relevant to gas GOs include the introduction of hourly or sub-hourly timestamps for gas GOs, divisibility of GOs into fractions that are multiples of 1 Wh, simplified registration for installations under 50 kW, extension of the right to receive GOs for RFNBOs including hydrogen, and a new requirement for the Commission to report on options for a Union-wide green label by 31 December 2025 [10]. RED III also removed an exception that had allowed Member States to avoid requiring GOs for electricity supplier fuel mix disclosure, thereby strengthening the mandatory use of the GO system [10].

Crucially, RED III did not alter the core decoupling principle. The fundamental architecture -- that GOs can be transferred independently of the energy to which they relate -- remains intact and was reaffirmed through the extension to new energy carriers [10].

## 5. Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/996: The Mass Balance Mechanism

### 5.1 The EU Gas Grid as a Single Mass Balance Site

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/996, adopted on 14 June 2022, lays down rules for verifying sustainability and greenhouse gas emissions savings criteria and for low indirect land-use change-risk criteria [3]. Its provisions on mass balancing are the operational backbone enabling the transfer of sustainability attributes through the gas grid.

**Recital 5** of IR 2022/996 states that, for gaseous fuels, the EU interconnected grid is considered one mass-balancing system [3]. That supports administrative transfer within a qualifying chain, but it does not by itself prove that any gas withdrawal or LNG cargo carries a valid compliance PoS. The operator still has to document the sustainability characteristics and preserve the applicable mass-balance and certification records.

### 5.2 Definition of Interconnected Infrastructure

**Article 2(18)** of IR 2022/996 defines "interconnected infrastructure" as a system including pipelines, LNG terminals and storage facilities that transports gases that can technically and safely be injected into and transported through the natural-gas pipeline system [3]. The explicit inclusion of LNG terminals makes them potentially relevant to a mass-balance chain; it does not, standing alone, make fossil LNG a compliant renewable fuel.

### 5.3 How Mass Balance Works in Practice

**Article 19(2)(d)** specifies when fuels introduced into an interconnected logistical facility or transmission/distribution infrastructure can be treated as part of a mixture for mass-balance purposes. It also requires the sustainability and GHG characteristics and other fuel information to be documented and passed through the supply chain [3]. The provision therefore supports reassignment inside a documented chain; it is not a general licence to relabel fossil gas or LNG.

The Commission's current UDB description says that injected gaseous-fuel volumes are verified by TSOs or DSOs. Intermediary trades within the integrated grid are not formally recorded in the same way as other supply-chain transactions, but Transfer Gas PoS moves the relevant volumes and PoS between UDB accounts, with a formal transaction recorded at the exit or final-consumption point [26]. Whether a particular LNG withdrawal can carry a compliance PoS therefore depends on the applicable infrastructure, mass-balance records, certification scheme and end-use rules. It cannot be inferred from Article 2(18)'s inclusion of LNG terminals alone.

## 6. The Book-and-Claim Mechanism: How GOs Enable Relabeling

### 6.1 Two Distinct but Related Mechanisms

The EU framework operates through two distinct certificate mechanisms that are often conflated but serve different regulatory purposes.

**Guarantees of Origin (GOs)** under Article 19 RED II serve a disclosure function: they allow end consumers to claim their energy consumption is "renewable" for marketing, ESG reporting, and voluntary sustainability purposes. GOs operate on a strict book-and-claim basis -- the certificate is completely decoupled from the physical molecule [1][2].

**Proofs of Sustainability (PoS)** under the sustainability certification scheme (Article 30 RED II, IR 2022/996) serve a compliance function: they demonstrate that fuel in a documented chain meets sustainability and GHG criteria for the relevant regulatory use. PoS operate on a mass-balance basis within qualifying interconnected infrastructure -- not full book-and-claim, but not molecule-level physical tracing either [3]. The distinction matters: an independently transferred GO is not a substitute for a compliance PoS. The May 2025 Energy Traders Europe position paper argues that physical gas trade should be a prerequisite for PoS transfer; that is an industry position rather than binding law, but it reinforces the need to distinguish a physical/mass-balance chain from a disclosure-only claim [29].

The ERGaR (European Renewable Gas Registry) operates both mechanisms. Its Certificate of Origin (CoO) scheme enables cross-border transfer of biomethane certificates, and since 2021 has "facilitated more than 7 TWh in biomethane between the participants" [11]. ERGaR states explicitly that "These certificates can be traded irrespective of the physical flow of the renewable gas via a so-called book and claim system" [11]. However, one constraint applies: "when both a Proof of Sustainability and Guarantee of Origin exist for the same consignment of gas, they cannot be traded separately to different companies" [11].

### 6.2 The LNG Pathway

The conditional pathway by which biomethane certificates may be associated with gas or LNG operates as follows.

**Step 1: Production and Injection.** A biomethane producer injects certified biomethane into the EU gas grid and receives a GO (for disclosure) and/or a PoS (for compliance) from a national registry or recognised voluntary scheme.

**Step 2: Certificate Trading.** The GO can be sold to a buyer independently of the physical gas for disclosure purposes. A PoS can move only through the applicable certification, mass-balance and UDB rules; the current UDB documentation describes Transfer Gas PoS movements within the integrated grid [26].

**Step 3: Application to LNG.** An LNG terminal may fall within the definition of interconnected infrastructure, but that fact alone does not establish that a fossil LNG purchase qualifies as compliant bio-LNG. The operator must show the required chain, scheme, UDB and end-use records. Separately, a company purchasing fossil LNG may purchase and cancel a biomethane GO for a disclosure claim, subject to the rules governing that claim.

**Step 4: Consumer-facing claim.** The end consumer may see a renewable disclosure claim even though the physically consumed molecules differ from the molecules that generated the GO. That disclosure result must not be presented as proof that the same fossil LNG was a compliant renewable-fuel consignment.

The bio-LNG producer Nordsol has documented this distinction sharply: in "mass-balanced bio-LNG and greened LNG, the methane molecules are fossil but carry the green label of a biomethane molecule" [12]. Nordsol warns that "Greening fossil LNG by relabeling or offsetting it as 'bio' crumbles the integrity of the bio-LNG label" [12].

## 7. ENTSOG and Industry Positions

### 7.1 ENTSOG's Advocacy for Gas GOs

ENTSOG (European Network of Transmission System Operators for Gas) has been the leading industry advocate for extending GOs to gas. Since 2018, ENTSOG and GIE (Gas Infrastructure Europe) have co-chaired the "Prime Movers Group" on GOs, bringing together gas industry experts and GO issuing bodies [13].

ENTSOG's March 2021 position paper on the RED II revision proposed that GOs for all energy carriers "should indicate the carbon footprint of the produced energy, ensuring that the GO could be used as a common carbon currency and help kick-start the European climate market" [13]. ENTSOG advocates that "issuance of GOs to low-carbon gases should become an obligation for Member States rather than an option, to promote the development of low-carbon gases via a common harmonised EU framework and prevent regulatory fragmentation and market distortions" [13].

ENTSOG's legal analysis document from April 2019 examined the scope and principles of GOs under RED II, supporting the view that the book-and-claim system should be applied to gas in the same manner as electricity [14].

### 7.2 The AIB and EECS Gas GO Scheme

The Association of Issuing Bodies (AIB), which administers the European Energy Certificate System (EECS), has extended its platform to support gas GOs. The EECS Gas GO Scheme "fully facilitates guarantees of origin for gaseous energy carriers (including hydrogen) under Article 19 of the Renewable Energy Directive 2018/2001, and their effective cross-border transfer" [15]. The AIB's analysis of RED III changes confirmed that the amendments reinforce the GO system while extending it to new carriers [10].

## 8. Court Decisions: CJEU Case C-573/12 Alands Vindkraft

### 8.1 The Ruling

The Court of Justice of the European Union delivered a landmark ruling on 1 July 2014 in Case C-573/12, Alands Vindkraft AB v Energimyndigheten [5]. The case concerned a Finnish wind farm operator on the Aland Islands connected to the Swedish power grid, whose application for Swedish green certificates was rejected because the facility was not located in Sweden.

The Grand Chamber ruled that Member States are not required to open national renewable energy support schemes to producers in other EU countries, finding this territorial limitation justified under EU law [5]. The ruling made a critical distinction about GOs: "the guarantees of origin set forth by Article 15 RED are not capable of providing proper RES verification since their sole purpose is to indicate to final customers the proportion of energy from renewable sources in an electricity supplier's energy mix" [5].

### 8.2 Implications for Gas GOs

The Alands Vindkraft ruling established that GOs are purely disclosure instruments, not compliance instruments. They tell the consumer where the energy was produced but cannot guarantee that the consumer actually received that energy. This distinction is fundamental to understanding the gas GO system: when a company purchases a biomethane GO and cancels it, EU law does not claim that the company actually consumed biomethane. It claims only that somewhere, biomethane was produced, and the company has the right to disclose that production as part of its energy mix. The physical molecules the company consumed may be entirely fossil.

This legal architecture was explicitly designed and upheld by the CJEU. It is not a loophole -- it is the system working as intended.

## 9. Critical Analysis: Greenwashing and the Limits of Book-and-Claim

### 9.1 Academic and NGO Criticism

The decoupling of GOs from physical energy has attracted sustained criticism. The core objection is that GOs "risk enabling greenwashing -- offering the appearance of sustainability without the substance," allowing companies to "continue consuming fossil-based power while purchasing GOs to claim '100% renewable' operations on paper" [16]. This concern applies with equal or greater force to gas GOs than to electricity GOs because, unlike electrons on an integrated grid, gas molecules can be physically segregated and traced through the supply chain.

Academic literature on the environmental integrity of certificate systems has documented the "additionality" problem: certificates are frequently "issued for power plants that already exist or that have been subsidized by other means" without guaranteeing new renewable capacity [7]. In the gas context, this means biomethane GOs may not drive any additional biomethane production beyond what regulation or subsidies already mandate.

The journal Environmental Justice published research characterising the "greenwashing of biogas as a solution to the environmental hazards associated with CAFOs" as "an example of legalized pollution" [17]. While this critique targets the feedstock side rather than the certification mechanism, it highlights broader concerns about the environmental credentials of the biomethane supply chain.

### 9.2 The Iceland Precedent

The electricity GO market has already produced a cautionary example. Iceland, which generates nearly all electricity from renewable sources, was exporting GOs to continental European buyers while domestic companies simultaneously claimed 100% renewable consumption. The AIB suspended Icelandic GO exports in April 2023 over double-counting concerns, later lifting the ban while acknowledging unresolved issues [7]. Norway faces similar concerns. This precedent demonstrates that book-and-claim systems can produce outcomes where the aggregate renewable claims exceed the actual renewable production.

### 9.3 Member State Resistance

The Netherlands is a live example of the unresolved boundary between EU mass balance and national compliance design. Its original 2024 TRIS notification temporarily limited eligibility to Dutch production and physical injection into the Dutch network. The Netherlands later deleted those restrictions after the Commission's detailed opinion. In its latest exchange of 26 June 2026, the Commission viewed the deletions positively but continued to ask whether foreign EU gas is now eligible, whether physical transport remains required, and how the GGE register, AIB/VertiCer route, GO/PoS coupling and UDB interact [27]. The Commission has reserved its position; this is an unresolved regulatory dispute, not evidence that the Netherlands currently has a settled domestic-only rule.

The current Dutch draft implementing order requires a qualifying delivery to carry both a GO and a PoS in the same mass-balance-traceable chain and excludes gas receiving an operating subsidy, including foreign gas [28]. Those conditions may create practical constraints, but they are different from a formal blanket prohibition on biomethane imports.

### 9.4 The Cross-Border Trading Gap

Cross-border GO trading is legally available, but the existence of a transferable GO does not establish that a corresponding PoS can be used in every national compliance scheme. The relevant evidence for any cross-border compliance claim is the linked physical/mass-balance chain, UDB record, voluntary-scheme documentation and national eligibility rule.

## 10. The Florence School of Regulation Analysis

The Florence School of Regulation (FSR) at the European University Institute has produced the most substantive academic analysis of gas GOs. A May 2020 workshop directed by Alberto Pototschnig and Ilaria Conti examined "which role Guarantees of Origin might play in promoting the development of renewable gases within a framework aimed at achieving renewable penetration policy goals at least costs" [19].

The FSR's key conclusion was that GOs should be upgraded into a "common currency" instrument performing multiple roles: supporting all energy vectors beyond electricity, becoming statutory rather than voluntary, and enabling optimal renewable energy mix achievement at least cost [19]. Participants "do not see a problem in Guarantees of Origin performing multiple roles" and would support GOs "being used across vectors and across jurisdictions" [19].

However, the FSR also identified significant risks. The workshop noted a "lack of coordination between different mechanisms could undermine effectiveness and efficiency" and stressed "the importance of defining the scope of upgraded GOs and the linkage with the ETS, together with verification and sustainability equivalence" [19]. The observation that "the same unit of energy could be 'certified' for more than one purpose (renewable, decarbonised, local)" points to the risk of overlapping and potentially contradictory certification claims [19].

A separate FSR publication, "Upgrading Guarantees of Origin to Promote the Achievement of the EU Renewable Energy Target at Least Cost," explored the concept of Guarantees of Renewable Origin (GROs) as a more robust alternative to traditional GOs, though this work focused primarily on electricity rather than gas [20].

## 11. The CEN EN 16325 Standard Extension

The technical standard underpinning GO systems, CEN EN 16325, was originally limited to electricity. In 2020, CEN initiated a revision to extend its scope to "electricity, gaseous hydrocarbons, Hydrogen, and heating & cooling" [8]. After five years of deliberation, the revised standard was accepted by weighted majority vote in CEN in March 2025, covering all EU and EEA member states [8].

The extension of CEN EN 16325 to gas means that gas GOs will be issued, traded, and cancelled using the same standardised electronic format as electricity GOs, reinforcing the fungibility of renewable energy claims across vectors. However, a transitional period is in effect, and deadlines for national registry implementation have not yet been finalised [8].

## 12. Synthesis and Legal Assessment

### 12.1 The Legal Chain of Provisions

The legal chain should be stated in two separate tracks: a broad disclosure rule for GOs, and a conditional mass-balance route for compliance PoS.

1. **Recital 53, RED II** (Directive 2018/2001): Establishes that a GO "can be transferred, independently of the energy to which it relates" [1].

2. **Article 19(2), RED II**: Allows transfers "separately or together with the physical transfer of energy" [2].

3. **Recital 59, RED II**: Extends GOs from electricity to "renewable gas" [1].

4. **Article 19(7), RED II**: Mandates conformity with CEN EN 16325, now extended to gas [2][8].

5. **Recital 5, IR 2022/996**: Treats the EU interconnected gas grid as one mass-balancing system for gaseous fuels [3].

6. **Article 2(18), IR 2022/996**: Defines interconnected infrastructure to include pipelines, LNG terminals and storage facilities [3].

7. **Article 19(2)(d), IR 2022/996**: Allows qualifying fuels in interconnected infrastructure to be treated as part of a mixture for mass-balance purposes, subject to documentation and chain-of-custody rules [3].

8. **Directive 2024/1788**: Extends GO rules to low-carbon gas, providing that low-carbon gas GOs "shall be subject to the same rules as those set out for guarantees of origin issued for the production of renewable gas" [4].

9. **Directive 2023/2413 (RED III)**: Amends Article 19 to strengthen GO requirements without altering the decoupling principle [10].

### 12.2 Is This Greenwashing?

Under EU law, GOs are disclosure instruments that may be transferred independently of physical energy. That does not mean PoS can be freely detached from the mass-balance and certification chain. The current UDB documentation and Dutch draft show active controls and unresolved implementation questions.

The strongest criticism is therefore narrower: a company may be able to buy and cancel a GO while consuming different gas and make a disclosure claim. A stronger claim that the same fossil LNG has been accepted as a compliant renewable-fuel consignment requires evidence of the applicable PoS, mass-balance, UDB and end-use rules. The documents reviewed do not establish that as a settled general legal fact.

The critical question is whether this administrative equivalence delivers actual environmental benefit. Proponents argue that GO revenue provides a financial incentive for biomethane production, thereby driving investment in renewable gas capacity. Critics counter that the premium captured by GOs is too small to drive additionality and that the system primarily enables low-cost compliance and marketing claims by fossil gas consumers.

## Recommendations

1. **For investigative journalism**: Use Recital 53 and Article 19(2) of RED II to document GO disclosure decoupling. Use IR 2022/996, the UDB documentation and the applicable national rules to test any PoS or LNG compliance claim. Do not treat LNG's inclusion in the infrastructure definition as proof that fossil LNG is compliant bio-LNG.

2. **For policy analysis**: The Netherlands is the clearest live case: its original domestic-production and physical-injection restrictions were deleted, but the Commission continues to examine foreign EU gas, physical transport, AIB/VertiCer, GGE, GO/PoS and UDB questions [27]. The current Dutch draft also excludes operating-subsidized gas and requires GO/PoS coupling [28].

3. **For corporate accountability**: The Nordsol distinction between "fossil-free bio-LNG" (actual biomethane molecules) and "greened LNG" (fossil molecules with certificates) provides a clear framework for evaluating corporate green gas claims.
