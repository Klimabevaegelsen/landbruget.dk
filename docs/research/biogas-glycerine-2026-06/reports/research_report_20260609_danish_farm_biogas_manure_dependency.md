## Executive Summary

Denmark's largest livestock farms have become structurally locked into a dependency on subsidised biogas plants for manure disposal — a dependency so deep that removing the biogas channel would force measurable herd reductions and render hundreds of farms legally non-compliant overnight. This report synthesises regulatory analysis, farm-level economics, international comparisons, and counterfactual modelling to show that biogas is not merely a convenient outlet for manure but the load-bearing infrastructure that keeps Denmark's intensive livestock sector legal.

The mechanism is regulatory, not voluntary. Danish harmony rules cap manure nitrogen application at 170 kg N/ha (previously 230 for cattle farms, until the derogation ended in July 2024) [1]. Farms exceeding this ceiling must export manure or shrink their herds. Our prior quantitative work established that 590 biogas-supplier farms produce 12 million kg N above what their land can absorb — roughly 6% of the national herd's manure — and biogas absorbs 182% of that excess [2]. Without biogas, these farms face a binding legal constraint with no adequate alternative at current scale.

The economics confirm the lock-in. Danish biogas plants accept manure at zero cost to the farmer (a tradition called "nulpris"), and farmers receive digestate back with 15-30% improved fertiliser value [3][4]. The Netherlands, which lost its equivalent derogation in 2023-2025, now sees manure disposal costs of €25-30/m³ (up from €7 in 2021), with 87% of dairy farms producing more manure than they can use [5][6]. A comparable cost shock in Denmark would add 500-800 million DKK annually to the bill for the 2,200 biogas-supplier farms alone.

The dependency is deepening. The share of exported manure-N going to biogas grew from 24.5% in 2018 to 39.2% in 2022, and the cattle derogation ending in July 2024 pushed an additional 1 million tonnes of manure into the system, with farmers told their options are: buy 30,000 extra hectares of land, reduce cows, or send more to biogas [7][8]. Denmark's climate plan (Grøn Trepart) and CO2 tax from 2030 further entrench biogas as the expected disposal route [9]. The trap is closing: farms designed around biogas export have no viable exit, while the biogas plants they depend on are increasingly owned by Shell and private equity funds operating as local monopsonies [10].

## 1. Introduction

### Scope and Purpose

This report investigates a specific question at the heart of Denmark's agricultural-industrial complex: what would happen to the country's biggest livestock farms if the biogas channel for manure disposal disappeared? The question matters because Denmark spends approximately 2.4 billion DKK per year in climate-labelled subsidies on biogas [11], and our prior quantitative analysis has shown that the primary beneficiaries are the same ~2,200 intensive farms that already collect ~1.3 billion DKK in CAP agricultural support [2]. If these farms are structurally dependent on the subsidised disposal route — not merely benefiting from it — then the subsidy is not a climate measure in any meaningful sense. It is an operating licence for intensive livestock production, funded from the climate budget.

### What We Already Know

The Landbruget.dk investigation has established five quantified findings from Danish public data (Gødningsregnskab × Energistyrelsen × virk.dk, manure year 2022):

- **B-1 (Paid Twice):** 2,202 farms supply biogas; 87% collect both CAP subsidy and a biogas-subsidy share. Combined public support: ~3.65 billion DKK/year [2].
- **B-2 (Who Banks It):** Foreign/PE owners now receive ~50% of the biogas subsidy. Shell alone: 440 million DKK (19%). Danish farmer cooperatives, who built the fleet, retain 6% [10].
- **B-3 (Lock-In):** 90% of supplier farms deliver to exactly one plant — a local monopsony [10].
- **B-4 (Environmental Payload):** ~1 in 5 geocoded supplier farms sit in a groundwater protection zone [2].
- **B-5 (Afloat or Extend):** 590 over-ceiling farms hold 19% of the national herd. Biogas absorbs 182% of their manure excess. Supplier herds grew +2% (over-ceiling: +10%) while non-suppliers shrank -6% [2].

This report extends those findings by asking: *how deep is the trap?*

### Methodology

Seven research dimensions are investigated through web search, document analysis, regulatory review, and international comparison: (1) the Danish regulatory framework that creates the dependency; (2) the economics of disposal alternatives; (3) physical and logistical constraints; (4) international parallels (Netherlands, Flanders, Brittany, Germany); (5) counterfactual modelling of subsidy removal; (6) the trajectory of deepening dependency; and (7) the policy feedback loop that makes escape harder over time.

## 2. The Regulatory Lock-In: Danish Law Makes Biogas Indispensable

### 2.1 The Harmony Rules — A Nitrogen Ceiling That Binds

The foundation of Denmark's manure management system is the "harmoniregler" (harmony rules), rooted in the EU Nitrates Directive (91/676/EEC) and implemented through the Husdyrgødningsbekendtgørelsen. These rules impose a hard ceiling on the amount of organic nitrogen that may be applied per hectare of agricultural land: **170 kg N/ha** as the general limit [1]. For 22 years (2002-2024), Danish cattle farms enjoyed a derogation allowing **230 kg N/ha** on grassland-rich farms, in exchange for maintaining two-thirds grass cover [1][7].

In practice, the harmony rules tie herd size to land area. A farm may keep no more than 0.90 dairy cows per hectare, or 62.9 fattening pigs per hectare [12]. Any manure produced above the ceiling must be exported — either to another farm with spare harmony area, or to a biogas plant that accepts it and returns digestate to farms with remaining capacity. The Frontiers in Sustainability study documenting Danish manure regulation confirms that "the amount of nitrogen on a farm may be reduced by export of manure to biogas plants or to fellow farmers having a need for the plant nutrients" [12].

The enforcement mechanism operates through the **gødningsregnskab** (fertiliser account), which every agricultural enterprise must file annually. The account reports nitrogen quota, mineral N use, crop production, and manure production and disposition [12]. Farmers who exceed their quota face fines, and the Danish Agricultural Authority (Landbrugsstyrelsen) has a duty to report non-compliant farms to the police [13]. EU cross-compliance rules add a further sanction: CAP subsidies can be reduced for farms that breach nitrogen limits [12]. Slaughterhouse and dairy data are cross-checked against reported animal numbers, making systematic fraud detectable [12].

The Rigsrevisionen (National Audit Office) has identified gaps in enforcement oversight, but the underlying compliance is strong: Danish farmers voluntarily use only 92-96% of their nitrogen quotas on average, and aggregate overfertilisation amounts to only 2-3% of total nitrogen input [14]. The enforcement structure is credible: fines of 10 DKK/kg N for the first 30 kg N/ha of overage, escalating to 20-30 DKK/kg for larger excesses. A police report (politianmeldelse) is filed automatically when per-hectare overage exceeds 10 kg N [41][42]. For a 500-cow dairy farm exceeding by 20 kg N/ha across 400 hectares, the fine alone would be ~80,000 DKK, plus criminal liability. The Højesteret (Supreme Court) confirmed in 2019 that nitrogen quota violations are criminally enforceable [42]. The system works not because enforcement is draconian but because the rules are internalised — and because biogas provides a cost-free exit valve.

### 2.2 The Cattle Derogation Ends: July 2024 Shock

On 31 July 2024, the Danish cattle derogation ended — the single most consequential regulatory tightening for farm-biogas dependency in a generation [7]. After 22 years of allowing dairy farms to apply 230 kg N/ha, every farm in Denmark now faces the 170 kg N/ha general ceiling. Denmark is the only EU member state that chose not to seek renewal of its derogation from 2026 onwards [15].

The impact numbers are stark. Approximately **32% of Danish milk producers** used the derogation, but because it was concentrated in larger operations, **~45% of all Danish dairy cows** were on affected farms [7][8][43]. These farms now need **30,000 additional hectares of spreading area** — land they do not own [8]. **One million tonnes of manure** must be relocated [8]. Individual farms must find up to **35% more spreading area** to remain compliant [8]. For a concrete 500-cow dairy farm on 300 hectares, the arithmetic is devastating: N excretion of ~149 kg N per dairy cow (2020/21 norms) means 74,500 kg N total. Under the old 230 ceiling, 324 hectares sufficed. At 170, the farm needs 438 hectares — **114 extra hectares, a 35% increase** [43].

The options are limited: buy or rent additional land (at a time when the Grøn Trepart agreement is retiring 140,000 hectares of carbon-rich lowland), reduce the herd, or **send more manure to biogas** [7][8]. The dairy farmer organisation LDM (Landsforeningen af Danske Mælkeproducenter) called the decision "incomprehensible and unacceptable," noting it came with "extremely short notice" and without the phased transition that Ireland or the Netherlands attempted [8]. Biogas Danmark itself warned of falling biogas production and "million-kroner bills" in 2025, precisely because the derogation ending disrupts existing manure flows [16].

The net effect is unambiguous: the derogation ending pushes *more* manure toward biogas at the exact moment when the system is already operating near capacity. Farms that were marginal before — just under the 230 ceiling — now face a binding constraint at 170. Biogas is their lifeline.

### 2.3 The Environmental Permit: Biogas Written Into the Licence

Danish livestock farms above certain ammonia-emission thresholds require a **miljøgodkendelse** (environmental permit) from the municipality. The permit specifies maximum animal units, ammonia limits, and — critically — manure disposition plans [17]. The Husdyrgodkendelsesloven and its implementing bekendtgørelse require that "sufficient agreements on manure disposal must be in place" as a condition of the permit [17].

In practice, this means that a farm expanding or maintaining a large herd must demonstrate, at the time of permit application, where its excess manure will go. If the answer is "biogas plant X," the permit is granted on that assumption. If the biogas plant later stops accepting manure — because it closes, reaches capacity, or changes ownership — the farm's permit conditions are no longer met. The farm faces a choice between finding an alternative disposal route (which may not exist locally) or reducing its herd to fit within its own harmony area.

Miljøstyrelsen's helpdesk guidance confirms that farms with biogas delivery agreements can reduce their on-site slurry storage capacity below the standard 9-month requirement — but only if the biogas plant provides guaranteed off-take [18]. Critically, these manure disposal agreements (gylleafsætningsaftaler) must have a **minimum 5-year duration** under Husdyrgødningsbekendtgørelsen §11, stk. 4 [44]. This creates a physical dependency on top of the regulatory one: farms that reduced storage investment because they relied on regular biogas collection cannot simply switch to storing and spreading manure on their own land. They lack the tank capacity. And the 5-year contract term locks them in even if conditions change.

### 2.4 The 9-Month Storage Requirement

Danish law requires farms to maintain slurry storage capacity for a minimum of 9 months [12]. For a large dairy farm producing 10,000 m³ of slurry per year, this means 7,500 m³ of tank capacity — a capital investment of approximately €70,000 at current costs [12]. Farms that have contracted with biogas plants for regular collection may have been permitted to build smaller tanks. If the biogas channel closes, they face both a capital investment to expand storage *and* the immediate question of where to spread the stored slurry during the spreading season (February-September, matching the Dutch calendar).

The seasonal dimension matters: Denmark bans autumn application to bare soil, and 92% of liquid manure is now spring-applied (up from 55% in 1990) [12]. This concentrates disposal into a narrow window, making the biogas route even more important as a year-round off-take that smooths seasonal pressure.

## 3. The Economics of Disposal: Free Today, Ruinous Without Biogas

### 3.1 The "Nulpris" Tradition — Farms Pay Nothing

Danish biogas plants have traditionally accepted manure at zero cost to the farmer — the "nulpris" (zero price) tradition [3][4]. This is not an accident but a feature of the cooperative model: farmers built the plants together, delivered manure as a condition of membership, and received digestate back with improved fertiliser properties. The economic logic was that the biogas plant captured value from the energy content (subsidised gas revenue) while the farmer captured value from free disposal and better-quality return fertiliser.

SEGES Innovation's analysis argues that farmers *should* be receiving 10-20 DKK per tonne for manure delivered to biogas, because manure remains "among the most economically attractive raw materials for biogas" — yet the price has not adjusted upward despite rising input prices elsewhere [3]. The farmer organisation LBBL (Landbrugets Biomasseleverandører) has demanded at least **14 DKK/tonne**, arguing that farmers supply 75% of biogas plant feedstock yet receive nothing while biogas production generates 11 DKK/m³ for operators (including 3 DKK/m³ in government subsidies) [47]. A 2022 breakthrough agreement between seven supplier associations and Nature Energy tied payment to gas prices and climate certificate prices — but specific amounts were never disclosed publicly, and negotiations with Bigadan and E.ON remained unresolved [47]. This reflects the power imbalance identified in our B-3 finding: with 90% of supplier farms locked to a single local plant, farmers have no competitive buyer. The plant (increasingly Shell or Bigadan/Arjun) sets the terms.

The digestate return is the farmer's main tangible benefit — but even this is deteriorating. Historically, anaerobic digestion improved fertiliser value by making 5-8 kg more N available per livestock unit and reduced nitrate leaching by 2-4 kg per livestock unit [4][12]. However, SEGES now warns that "afgasset gylle er ikke altid det bedste på marken" (digestate is not always best for the field): as plants add straw, silage, and industrial residues, the ammonium share of total N has fallen from ~80% to only 67%, and N utilisation has declined to 50-70% from the historical 70-80% [48]. The net economic effect now ranges from a gain of 14 DKK/ha to a **loss of ~300 DKK/ha** depending on the situation [48]. The 15-30% fertiliser cost savings SEGES cited historically [4] are no longer guaranteed. Even the digestate "benefit" is eroding, leaving pure disposal relief as the dominant reason farmers stay locked in.

### 3.2 What Disposal Would Cost Without Biogas

The Netherlands provides the clearest real-world benchmark for what happens when manure disposal becomes a market transaction rather than a subsidised free good.

**Current Dutch costs (2024-2025):**
- Pig manure collection fee: **€33/m³** (up from €19/m³ in 2022 — a 73% increase) [5][6]
- Cattle manure disposal: **€30.55/m³** (up from €15/m³ in 2022 — doubled) [5][6]
- A typical mixed farm (2,200 pigs, 100 dairy cows) without enough spreading land: **€70,000-80,000/year** in manure disposal costs [6]
- Projected costs: **€40-50/m³** as derogation fully expires [5]

For comparison, Danish biogas-supplier farms deliver the same volume at zero cost and receive fertiliser back. The SEGES-cited figure for historical Danish farm-to-farm manure transfer is 10-20 DKK/tonne (~€1.3-2.7/tonne), but this assumes willing receivers with spare harmony area exist locally [3]. As the cattle derogation ending tightens available harmony area nationally, the price of bilateral transfer agreements will rise.

**Applying Dutch-level costs to Denmark:**
The 2,202 biogas-supplier farms deliver approximately 56 million kg N to biogas annually [2]. Assuming a slurry volume of roughly 20-25 million m³ (at typical nitrogen concentrations of 2-3 kg N/m³), Dutch-level disposal costs of €25-30/m³ would translate to **€500-750 million per year** (~3.7-5.5 billion DKK). Even at the historical Danish farm-to-farm rate of 10-20 DKK/tonne, the cost across 20+ million tonnes would be **200-400 million DKK/year** — but this assumes receiving farms exist with spare capacity, which is increasingly doubtful.

### 3.3 Alternative Disposal Technologies

**Farm-to-farm transfer:** The lowest-cost alternative, but constrained by available harmony area. With Denmark's total manure-N production around 230,000 tonnes/year [19] and 2.37 million hectares of arable land [20], the theoretical national capacity at 170 kg N/ha is ~403,000 tonnes — seemingly ample. But harmony area is not uniformly distributed. Western Jutland (where most pigs and dairy cows are concentrated) is saturated with manure, while eastern Denmark has more arable land relative to livestock. Transport costs of ~40-60 DKK/tonne beyond 20 km make long-distance transfer uneconomic [21].

**Manure separation:** Mechanical separation divides slurry into a phosphorus-rich solid fraction (exportable or combustible) and a nitrogen-rich liquid fraction (applicable locally). Treatment costs: **43-80 DKK/tonne** of manure; capital investment: 200,000-300,000 DKK for a low-cost solution; minimum viable volume: 10,000 tonnes/year [53]. Can reduce harmony area need by 25-50%, but as consultant Thorkild Birkmose concluded in LandbrugsAvisen: "Gylleseparation er for de fleste husdyrbrugere en dyr måde at løse harmoniproblemer" (manure separation is an expensive way to solve harmony problems for most livestock farmers) — land leasing or manure agreements with neighbours are generally cheaper [53]. Adoption remains limited because biogas makes it unnecessary for most large farms.

**Drying and pelletising:** Produces a concentrated, exportable fertiliser product. Very energy-intensive. The Danish company Daka ecoMotion processes organic waste but no large-scale manure pelletising industry exists in Denmark — because biogas has absorbed the demand.

**Incineration:** Danish law currently prohibits incineration of untreated animal manure [12]. Even if permitted, the energy recovery is poor relative to biogas and the ash-handling requirements are significant.

**Export:** The Netherlands exported 30% more manure in 2024, primarily dried poultry litter to Germany and France [6]. Denmark could theoretically export, but transport distances to receiving regions are longer, and the sheer volume (20+ million m³ annually) makes this impractical at scale.

The conclusion is inescapable: **no alternative disposal route exists at the scale and price point that biogas currently provides.** Every alternative is either more expensive, capacity-constrained, legally restricted, or logistically impractical for 56 million kg N per year. Biogas is not one option among many; it is the only option that works at the required scale.

## 4. The Dutch Mirror: What Denmark Would Look Like Without Biogas

### 4.1 The Netherlands Manure Crisis

The Netherlands provides an almost perfect natural experiment for Denmark's counterfactual. Dutch livestock density is even higher than Denmark's, the regulatory framework (EU Nitrates Directive) is identical, and the Netherlands is now living through the consequences of losing its manure derogation — the same derogation Denmark voluntarily abandoned in July 2024.

The Dutch derogation allowed application of 230-250 kg N/ha on grassland. It began phasing out in 2023, with nitrogen limits stepping down annually toward 170 kg N/ha by 2026 [5][22]. The anticipated surplus: **95,000 tonnes of nitrogen per year** by 2026, up from 15,000-30,000 tonnes in 2021-2022 [5].

The consequences have been immediate and severe:

**Cost explosion:** Manure disposal costs doubled from €15/m³ (2022) to €30/m³ (2024) for cattle, and tripled for pigs. For a standard dairy farm, annual disposal costs jumped from ~€20,000 to ~€40,000-60,000 [5][6]. This is *before* the derogation is fully phased out.

**Herd contraction:** The Dutch pig population fell to 9.96 million in 2025 — below 10 million for the first time in 45 years, a 5.1% year-on-year decline [45]. Cattle numbers dropped 3.3% in 2024 alone. Wageningen University projects loss of 167,000-450,000 dairy cattle (10-30% of the herd) by 2030 [5][6]. The number of pig farms fell to just 1,900 (down 7.1% year-on-year), while average herd size rose 50% since 2015 to ~5,000 pigs per farm — consolidation accelerating under cost pressure [45].

**Farm exits:** The government established two buyout schemes (LBV and LBV-plus) with a combined budget of **€2.92 billion**, targeting farms near Natura 2000 sites and peak emitters. By early 2026: €1.81 billion spent, 723 farms closed, achieving ~8% reduction in national nitrogen surplus [46]. Average cost: ~€2.5 million per closed farm. Investigative journalists found the scheme could have been €1.5 billion cheaper if only the few hundred largest emitters had been targeted [46]. A separate scheme "skims" 30% of animal rights on every farm sale, permanently reducing capacity [5].

**Manure fraud:** The surge in disposal costs created incentives for illegal dumping. Wikipedia's Dutch manure crisis article documents "widespread fraud among suppliers" and contamination problems [5].

**The key difference: Denmark has biogas.** The Netherlands did not develop a large-scale manure-to-biogas infrastructure comparable to Denmark's. Dutch biogas capacity is modest, and manure processing ("mestverwerking") technologies like separation and Renure are expensive (€22-38/tonne for Renure processing) [5]. Denmark's subsidised biogas sector is absorbing exactly the volume that, in the Dutch system, becomes an unaffordable disposal problem.

### 4.2 Flanders and Brittany: Further Parallels

**Flanders (Belgium):** Pig manure disposal costs reached €20-25/m³ by 2024, up from €7 in 2021 — an almost identical trajectory to the Netherlands [23]. Compliance costs for pig farms range from 0 to €2.66 per pig, driven primarily by manure export requirements [23]. The historic Manure Bank system was discontinued in 1998 after EU intervention.

**Brittany (France):** The consequences of intensive pig farming without adequate manure management are visible on the coastline. Brittany hosts 58% of French pig production (8,000 farms), and nitrate runoff causes toxic green algae (Ulva) proliferation that has permanently closed beaches [24]. The French government spent €134 million in 2010 alone on algae cleanup, and national public subsidies for nitrates programmes totalled €1.5 billion (1994-2001) and €560 million (2003-2007) [24]. Brittany demonstrates what happens at the *extreme* end: when neither biogas nor adequate regulation prevents nutrient overload, the environmental cost is paid in dead zones and public health emergencies.

### 4.3 Germany's Parallel Dependency

Germany's Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG) created a parallel farm-biogas dependency through the "Gülle-Bonus" (manure bonus), introduced in 2009. Plants needed a minimum 30% manure input to qualify [25]. The result: economically viable small biogas plants now require manure from at least 300 dairy cows or 17,000 hogs [25]. The current EEG incentivises only manure-based plants, deepening the structural linkage between livestock farming and biogas [25]. Germany's pattern confirms that wherever subsidies meet intensive livestock, the dependency relationship emerges — it is not unique to Denmark.

## 5. The Counterfactual: What If the Biogas Subsidy Stops?

### 5.1 The Immediate Binding Constraint

If Denmark's biogas plants stopped accepting manure tomorrow — whether due to subsidy removal, plant closures, or capacity constraints — the consequences for the 2,202 supplier farms would be immediate and severe:

**Step 1 — Legal non-compliance.** The 590 over-ceiling farms (producing 12 million kg N above their harmony capacity) would be instantly illegal [2]. They could not legally apply the excess on their own land. Every day of operation would constitute a violation of the Husdyrgødningsbekendtgørelsen, triggering fine liability and potential police reporting by Landbrugsstyrelsen [13].

**Step 2 — Storage crisis.** Farms that reduced storage capacity under biogas delivery agreements would face a physical overflow problem within weeks. With spreading banned in winter months and a 9-month storage requirement, farms with undersized tanks would need emergency disposal — but where? Neighbouring farms are already at or near their harmony ceiling.

**Step 3 — Forced herd reduction.** The only legal response for farms without adequate harmony area and without a disposal outlet is to reduce animal numbers to fit within the 170 kg N/ha ceiling. Our data shows this would affect ~6% of the national herd's manure production [2]. The actual herd reduction could be larger, because marginal farms near the ceiling would also need to contract.

**Step 4 — Fire-sale economics.** When 590+ farms simultaneously need to sell animals and/or buy/rent land, the market impact is asymmetric: animal prices crash (too many sellers) while land prices spike (too many buyers for harmony area). The Dutch experience confirms this: herd contraction became self-reinforcing as disposal costs made marginal farms unviable first, then spread to mid-size operations.

### 5.2 Has Anyone Modelled This?

No comprehensive counterfactual model of biogas subsidy removal has been published by the major Danish institutions. However, several partial assessments exist:

**Klimarådet (Danish Climate Council)** has repeatedly questioned biogas cost-effectiveness. Its 2024 Statusrapport warned that "more methane may be emitted from biogas production than previously assumed" and noted that Denmark's renewable-energy targets are being undermined by selling too many biogas origin guarantees abroad [26]. The 2024 background note on bioressourcer models manure-biogas interactions in climate scenarios but does not model subsidy removal [26].

**CONCITO** has been the sharpest critic. Its analysis found that greenhouse gas emissions from manure-based biogas are "at best on the same level as ordinary manure handling" — but only if methane leakage stays below 1% of methane production [27][28]. CONCITO further argued that the biogas subsidy (~100 DKK/GJ, equivalent to ~1,800 DKK/tonne CO₂ displaced) far exceeds proposed CO₂ tax rates and "undermines the green tax reform" by removing incentives for genuine emissions reduction [11].

**WRI (World Resources Institute)** concluded that Denmark's biogas subsidies are "cost-ineffective" and that "extending biogas facilities to all of Denmark's manure could require subsidies equal to the value of all Danish agriculture" [9]. WRI further noted that Denmark has "scaled back its financing for new biogas facilities" and identified "several promising, potentially cheaper, manure management technologies as priorities for trials" [9].

**IFRO (Institut for Fødevare- og Ressourceøkonomi, University of Copenhagen)** has published the most rigorous cost-effectiveness analysis. IFRO Rapport 220 (2013) calculated a societal CO₂ shadow price of **1,128 DKK/tonne CO₂** for biogas — a dramatic deterioration from a net *benefit* of 179 DKK/tonne in a 2005 analysis [49]. IFRO Rapport 271 (2018) found that processing 36% of manure through biogas costs taxpayers approximately **4.7 billion DKK in present value** (274-325 million DKK/year), equalling approximately **1,370 DKK/tonne CO₂e** reduced [49]. For comparison, IFRO found that retiring 44,700 hectares of carbon-rich lowland soils for extensive grazing costs only **~220 DKK/tonne CO₂e** — six times cheaper than biogas, with double the nitrogen reduction potential [49]. These numbers frame the dependency not just as a farm-level trap but as a societal misallocation: the most expensive climate measure sustains the most intensive production model.

### 5.3 Is There Spare Harmony Area?

Denmark has approximately 2.37 million hectares of arable land [20] and total manure-N production of ~230,000 tonnes/year [19]. At 170 kg N/ha, the theoretical national harmony capacity is ~403,000 tonnes — seemingly comfortable. But this calculation is misleading for three reasons:

**Geographic mismatch:** Livestock is concentrated in western and northern Jutland, while available harmony area without manure saturation is in eastern Denmark and the islands. Transport costs make distant disposal uneconomic. Our data shows a median transport distance of ~11 km for biogas deliveries; beyond 20-30 km, costs become prohibitive [21].

**Competing land demands:** The Grøn Trepart agreement will retire 140,000 hectares of carbon-rich lowland and establish 250,000 hectares of new forest by 2045 [9][30]. Both reduce available harmony area.

**Harmony area is already allocated.** Farms that are not biogas suppliers are using their own harmony area for their own manure. There is no large pool of unused receiving capacity waiting to absorb 56 million kg N from biogas-supplier farms. Farm-to-farm agreements exist, but expanding them at the scale needed to replace biogas would require hundreds of thousands of hectares of new bilateral arrangements.

### 5.4 The Implied Herd Reduction

If biogas disappeared and no alternative disposal route could absorb the full 56 million kg N, the implied herd reduction can be bounded. At the minimum, the 12 million kg N of "no legal home" manure [2] would need to be eliminated through destocking — equivalent to roughly 6% of the national herd. More realistically, because the remaining 44 million kg N of biogas-bound manure also lacks a guaranteed alternative outlet, the disruption would be broader.

Using Dutch parallels: Wageningen projects a 10-30% dairy herd reduction from the derogation loss alone [5]. Denmark's biogas dependency is structurally similar but even deeper (because Danish farms *relied* on biogas rather than facing the disposal cost directly). A conservative estimate: 10-15% reduction in the intensive livestock sector, concentrated in the largest pig and dairy operations in western Jutland.

## 6. The Deepening Dependency: A Ratchet That Only Turns One Way

### 6.1 The Growth Trajectory

Our own data from Danish fertiliser accounts shows the biogas share of exported manure-N has grown relentlessly:

| Year | To biogas (M kg N) | Total exported (M kg N) | Biogas share |
|------|--------------------:|------------------------:|-------------:|
| 2018 | 27.1 | 110.6 | 24.5% |
| 2019 | 35.7 | 121.2 | 29.4% |
| 2020 | 43.9 | 132.0 | 33.2% |
| 2021 | 51.6 | 142.1 | 36.3% |
| 2022 | 56.3 | 143.6 | 39.2% |

*Source: Gødningsregnskab analysis, ../scripts/disposition_over_time.csv*

By 2022, biogas was absorbing nearly 40% of all manure nitrogen that left the producing farm. The trend is accelerating: biogas-N nearly doubled in four years while other export channels grew modestly. Biogas Danmark's 2023 Outlook estimated that ~20% of Denmark's total livestock manure was already going to biogas, up from ~15% in 2018 [31]. The 2025 trajectory, accounting for the cattle derogation ending, likely pushes this to 25% or more.

### 6.2 The Derogation Shock Amplifies the Dependency

The cattle derogation ending in July 2024 is the single largest discrete intensifier of biogas dependency. Before the derogation, cattle farms could operate at 230 kg N/ha. After, the ceiling drops to 170 — a 26% reduction in per-hectare manure capacity. For a 500-cow dairy farm on 300 hectares, this means:

- **Before:** could apply 69,000 kg N on-farm (230 × 300)
- **After:** can apply 51,000 kg N on-farm (170 × 300)
- **Gap:** 18,000 kg N must be exported — equivalent to the manure from ~130 cows

This farm, previously legal, now has a manure surplus equivalent to roughly a quarter of its herd. It must find 18,000 kg N of disposal capacity. The three options (buy 106 extra hectares of land, remove 130 cows, or contract with biogas) overwhelmingly favour biogas: land is expensive and shrinking, herd reduction destroys the farm's revenue base, but biogas accepts the manure for free.

The aggregate effect: 40% of Danish dairy cows were on derogation farms, 30,000 hectares of additional spreading area are needed nationally, and 1 million tonnes of manure must be relocated [7][8]. Biogas is the only channel with the scale and willingness (at zero cost) to absorb this volume.

### 6.3 Farms Designed Around Biogas

A subtler dimension of the deepening dependency: new and expanding livestock farms are increasingly *designed* around the assumption of biogas export. When a farmer applies for a miljøgodkendelse to build a 1,000-cow dairy, the manure management plan assumes a significant fraction will go to biogas. The permit is granted on that basis. The farm's storage infrastructure is sized accordingly (potentially below the standard 9-month requirement, per Miljøstyrelsen guidance [18]). The investment in barns, milking equipment, and feeding infrastructure is sunk.

If biogas access later disappears, the farm cannot simply adapt. It would need to build storage, find harmony area, or destock — all after making a capital investment premised on biogas availability. This is the definition of a sunk-cost trap: the decision to build around biogas was rational at the time, but the reversibility is near-zero.

### 6.4 The CO₂ Tax Will Deepen the Trap

Denmark's Green Tripartite agreement introduces a CO₂ tax on agricultural emissions from 2030: 120 DKK/tonne CO₂e initially, rising to 300 DKK/tonne from 2035 (effective rates after a 60% deduction from nominal rates of 300/750 DKK) [9][30]. Per-cow annual cost: approximately €100 [30].

The tax creates a confirmed new incentive to route manure through biogas. DR News reported that the agreement "gør det smart at være klimavenlig landmand" (makes it smart to be a climate-friendly farmer), specifically citing biogas delivery as an approved climate technology alongside Bovaer feed additives [50]. **Sending manure to a biogas plant counts as an approved climate technology for the CO₂e tax**, eliminating 70-90% of methane emissions from manure storage [50]. For a farm with 800 dairy cows, this represents ~400 tonnes CO₂e/year of reduced emissions. At the 2035 effective rate (300 DKK/tonne), that is a potential **tax saving of ~120,000 DKK/year** per large farm [50]. Farms *not* sending manure to biogas face the full tax on manure methane emissions.

This turns the CO₂ tax into an accelerant of dependency: biogas participation directly reduces the tax bill, and the rational response is to send *all* manure to biogas — deepening the lock-in further. The total state subsidy commitment for biogas 2021-2030 is approximately **43 billion DKK** [51], with annual spending exceeding 2 billion DKK as of 2022 and expected to rise significantly.

CONCITO has identified this feedback loop explicitly, arguing that the biogas subsidy "undermines the green tax reform" because it removes the incentive for farms to actually *reduce* emissions rather than *route* them through a subsidised channel [11]. Klimarådet has separately warned that "kvægproduktionen kan ikke fortsætte på sit nuværende niveau" (livestock production cannot continue at current levels) to achieve the 55-65% emission reduction target [52].

### 6.5 The Certificate Crisis and Domestic Lock-In

Our prior research [documented locally in biogas_certificates_denmark_policy_findings_20260609.md] found that 88% of Danish biogas guarantees of origin (GOs) were purchased by foreign companies in 2024, primarily in Sweden and Germany [32]. That foreign revenue stream is exposed to changing national eligibility and certificate rules: GO disclosure transfers remain distinct from PoS-based compliance, and the current Dutch GGE proposal would require GO/PoS linkage and exclude operating-subsidized gas, while the Commission continues to question the final Dutch design [32][54][55]. Biogas Danmark has responded by lobbying for domestic demand mandates: a blending obligation on gas suppliers, tax exemption for "unsupported" biogas, and preservation of the gas grid and gas boilers [32].

If this domestic-demand strategy succeeds, Danish consumers become the captive market for biogas certificates — adding another layer of lock-in. The farms are locked to the plants; the plants are locked to the subsidy; and the subsidy is locked to domestic gas consumers and taxpayers. The entire chain becomes a closed loop of mutual dependency.

## 7. The Concentration Dimension: Who Gets Trapped and Who Benefits

### 7.1 The Largest Farms Are the Most Dependent

Our manure disposition data by herd-size quintile shows that biogas dependency scales monotonically with farm size:

| Quintile | Farms | Median produced N (tonnes) | Share to biogas |
|----------|------:|---------------------------:|----------------:|
| Q1 (smallest) | 2,751 | 0.1 | 1.0% |
| Q2 | 2,749 | 0.5 | 1.8% |
| Q3 | 2,749 | 1.3 | 4.6% |
| Q4 | 2,750 | 7.5 | 13.3% |
| **Q5 (largest)** | **2,750** | **37.7** | **32.6%** |

*Source: ../scripts/manure_disposition_2023.csv*

The largest quintile sends nearly a third of its manure to biogas. These are the 2,750 farms that produce the most manure, have the least land per animal unit, and are most constrained by harmony rules. They are, by definition, the most dependent on external disposal — and biogas is overwhelmingly the external route.

### 7.2 The Monopsony Problem

Our B-3 finding established that 90% of biogas-supplier farms deliver to exactly one plant [10]. This is structural, not a market failure: with manure transport economically viable only within ~20-30 km [21], and biogas plants spaced across the landscape, each farm typically has one local option.

The ownership pattern makes this a corporate monopsony. Shell's Nature Energy division controls ~13.3 million kg N of manure intake — the most of any owner group [10]. Bigadan (majority-owned by Arjun Infrastructure Partners, a PE fund) is the second-largest handler. Together, Shell and PE funds control roughly a third of all biogas-bound manure. These entities have no farming operations and no strategic interest in farmer welfare; they optimise for gas revenue and subsidy capture.

The farmer's negotiating position is clear: deliver manure to the one available plant at whatever terms are offered, or face an illegal manure surplus with no affordable alternative. The SEGES analysis confirming that farmers should receive 10-20 DKK/tonne but actually receive zero [3] is the market outcome of this monopsony.

### 7.3 Who Benefits From the Trap

The dependency structure creates three beneficiaries:

1. **Biogas plant owners** (increasingly Shell, PE, and foreign utilities) capture the subsidy stream (2.34 billion DKK/year) and the energy revenue from manure that comes to them for free. They acquired these positions by buying farmer cooperatives at a premium (Shell paid ~€2 billion for Nature Energy in 2022) and now collect the ongoing annuity [10].

2. **The largest farms** benefit from free disposal that would otherwise cost 200-800 million DKK/year collectively. Their herds remain legal and even expand. But the benefit is contingent — it exists only as long as the subsidy and the plant exist.

3. **Denmark's climate accounting** books methane-avoidance credits from manure-to-biogas, helping meet the 70% emissions reduction target for 2030. But CONCITO and Klimarådet question whether the actual climate benefit justifies the cost, given methane leakage concerns and the cost of ~1,800 DKK/tonne CO₂ displaced [11][27].

The losers are: Danish taxpayers (2.4 billion DKK/year and rising to 3.6 billion by 2030 [32]), the environment (nutrients are redistributed within catchments, not removed [2]), and medium-sized farms too small for biogas but competing for land and harmony area with subsidised giants.

## 8. Synthesis: The Self-Reinforcing Trap

The Danish farm-biogas dependency is not a single relationship but a system of interlocking reinforcements:

**Regulatory ratchet:** The cattle derogation ending (2024) and the CO₂ tax (2030) tighten the constraint that makes biogas necessary. Each tightening pushes more manure toward biogas. No regulatory change loosens the constraint.

**Economic ratchet:** Biogas accepts manure for free while all alternatives cost money. Farm investments (barns, milking parlours, feeding systems) are sunk on the assumption of biogas access. Exits are ruinously expensive. The nulpris tradition makes biogas seem costless, obscuring the dependency.

**Infrastructure ratchet:** Farms that reduced slurry storage under biogas agreements cannot quickly rebuild. Biogas plants are the only infrastructure with the capacity to handle 56 million kg N/year. No competing disposal industry exists at scale.

**Political ratchet:** Biogas Danmark lobbies to preserve gas boilers, the gas grid, and domestic certificate demand [32]. The climate plan assumes biogas expansion. The CO₂ tax drives farms toward biogas. Every policy step deepens the path dependency.

**Ownership ratchet:** Shell and PE funds own the plants; farmers are atomised suppliers. The cooperative model that gave farmers governance has been sold. Farmer-owners became supplier-dependents. The information asymmetry grows as professional managers replace local boards.

The Dutch experience shows where this leads without the biogas shock absorber: manure costs explode, herds contract involuntarily, and the government spends billions on buyouts. Denmark has delayed this reckoning by subsidising biogas — but the dependency means the reckoning, if it comes, will be even larger.

## 9. Limitations and Caveats

**Manure year lag:** The quantitative analysis uses 2022 manure data (2023 was partial at time of extraction). The cattle derogation ending in July 2024 means 2024-2025 data will show significantly higher dependency. Our numbers are therefore *conservative*.

**Upper-bound subsidy attribution:** Biogas subsidy is allocated to manure feedstock proportionally by N-share. Plants also process industrial waste and deep litter; the per-farm figure overstates manure-specific subsidy. However, manure earns the highest biomethane certificates precisely because it prevents the most methane, so the attribution is the subsidy's own logic.

**No formal counterfactual model:** No institution has published a complete model of what happens if Danish biogas subsidies are removed. The scenarios described in Section 5 are constructed from regulatory logic, Dutch/Flemish parallels, and economic reasoning — not from a calibrated equilibrium model.

**Causal claims:** The association between biogas supply and herd growth is correlational. Selection effects (growing farms self-select into biogas) cannot be excluded. The report frames biogas as *enabling* rather than *causing* expansion, consistent with the evidence.

**Geocoding coverage:** Environmental overlap analysis (B-4) covers 28% of supplier farms due to CHR geocoding gaps. The in-zone share (~20%) among geocoded farms is the headline; absolute numbers are lower bounds.

## 10. Recommendations for Further Investigation

1. **2024-2025 data update:** Re-run the B-5 (Afloat) analysis with post-derogation gødningsregnskab data to quantify the actual surge in biogas dependency.

2. **Miljøgodkendelse sample:** FOIA a sample of recent livestock environmental permits to verify whether biogas delivery is specified as a condition. This would establish the legal (not just economic) nature of the dependency.

3. **Biogas plant capacity utilisation:** Energistyrelsen data on actual vs. designed capacity for each plant would reveal whether the system is approaching saturation — the point at which new manure from derogation-loss farms cannot be absorbed.

4. **CO₂ tax interaction:** Model how the 2030 CO₂ tax changes the farm-level economics of biogas vs. alternative disposal. If the tax creates a larger incentive to use biogas, quantify the additional dependency.

5. **Harmony area mapping:** Use our existing field-level data to map available vs. saturated harmony area at the kommune level. This would show whether farm-to-farm transfer is a credible alternative or a theoretical possibility blocked by geographic concentration.

6. **Interview programme:** Current and former cooperative members who experienced the Shell/PE sale; Landbrugsstyrelsen enforcement officials; CONCITO/Klimarådet researchers on biogas cost-effectiveness.

38+ sources spanning: Danish legislation (retsinformation.dk, Miljøstyrelsen, Landbrugsstyrelsen), EU directives (Nitrates Directive, IED), government reports (Klimarådet, Energistyrelsen, CONCITO), peer-reviewed literature (Frontiers in Sustainability, IOP Science), industry publications (Biogas Danmark Outlook series, SEGES Innovation), international comparisons (USDA, WUR, Irish Examiner, EU Observer), and the Landbruget.dk investigation's own quantitative findings.
