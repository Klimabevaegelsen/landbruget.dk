# EU Treatment of LNG vs Piped Gas Carbon Footprint in ETS Accounting

## Executive Summary

For a standard EU ETS-covered installation, the EU does not apply a separate carbon-footprint penalty because gas arrived as LNG rather than by pipeline. The compliance obligation is based on direct emissions from the covered activity, normally fuel combusted multiplied by the relevant carbon dioxide emission factor and oxidation factor. The Monitoring and Reporting Regulation's standard methodology expresses combustion emissions as activity data for the amount of fuel combusted, based on net calorific value, multiplied by an emission factor and oxidation factor [3]. The same logic applies to ETS2 for buildings, road transport, and additional sectors, which covers carbon dioxide from fuel combustion rather than upstream lifecycle emissions [2].

There can still be small accounting differences between LNG-derived gas and pipeline gas if the operator uses measured or supplier-specific fuel composition, carbon content, net calorific value, or country-specific factors. That is a fuel-quality/composition difference, not a regulatory distinction that says "LNG has higher lifecycle CO2e than piped gas." In practice, once LNG is regasified and blended into the gas grid, the end-user ETS obligation usually tracks the gas combusted at the installation boundary, not the production route.

The EU does recognise that LNG can have a different lifecycle footprint outside ETS installation accounting. LNG's extra emissions are mainly upstream production methane, liquefaction energy use, shipping, and regasification. The IEA's 2025 LNG supply-chain analysis says LNG tends to have higher emissions than gas produced close to the point of consumption, while pipeline gas also varies with distance and leakage [8]. The EU Methane Regulation addresses methane from EU oil and gas operations, LNG facilities, and imported fossil energy supply chains, with importer reporting from 2025, MRV-equivalence obligations from 2027, methane-intensity reporting from 2028, and a methane-intensity requirement from 2030 [6]. This is not the same as an ETS carbon price on LNG's full well-to-burner footprint.

The main exception is maritime. EU ETS maritime starts with CO2 from ships from 2024 and adds methane and nitrous oxide from 1 January 2026 [7]. For ships using LNG as fuel, EU MRV rules include fugitive and slipped methane, expressed as a percentage of LNG fuel used by the emission source [10]. FuelEU Maritime, a separate maritime fuel-intensity regulation, goes further and uses well-to-wake accounting [11]. So LNG can be treated differently in maritime rules, but that does not change stationary EU ETS accounting for a gas plant or industrial boiler.

## Introduction

The research question was interpreted as: "Does EU ETS compliance accounting assign a higher CO2 or CO2e value to LNG than to pipeline natural gas, and where else in EU law is LNG's lifecycle footprint recognised?" Sources prioritised primary EU legal texts and official EU/agency guidance, then IEA lifecycle analysis for technical context.

## Main Analysis

### Finding 1: Standard EU ETS installation accounting is direct-emissions accounting

The European Commission describes the EU ETS as requiring covered companies to monitor and report annual emissions and surrender allowances to account for those emissions [1]. For stationary installations, the MRV page states that the monitoring plan is part of the greenhouse gas emissions permit, and operators prepare annual emissions reports that are verified and used for surrender obligations [2].

The core calculation rule is in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2018/2066. Under Article 24's standard methodology, combustion emissions are calculated from the amount of fuel combusted, expressed on a net calorific value basis, multiplied by an emission factor and oxidation factor [3]. Article 25's mass-balance methodology similarly accounts for carbon crossing the mass-balance boundary, converting carbon content to CO2 [3]. Article 31 lets default factors come from Annex VI, national inventory factors, competent-authority-approved literature values, supplier-guaranteed values, or representative historical analyses [4].

This means the compliance unit is not "LNG cargo" or "pipeline import route"; it is the fuel/source stream and its emissions at the regulated installation boundary. If two molecules of methane are combusted in the same turbine, their direct CO2 result is the same regardless of whether one molecule arrived via pipeline and the other via LNG import terminal. Any difference enters only through measured or accepted calculation factors such as NCV, density, carbon content, or emission factor.

### Finding 2: LNG's lifecycle premium is mostly outside ordinary ETS installation accounting

LNG has real upstream and midstream emissions that pipeline gas may not have, especially liquefaction, long-distance shipping, and regasification. The IEA's 2025 LNG report covers upstream production, processing, pipeline movement to export terminals, liquefaction, shipping, and regasification [8]. The same report states that extraction, processing, and transport average about 12 gCO2e/MJ for natural gas, while combustion emits about 55 gCO2/MJ; it also says LNG tends to have higher emissions than natural gas produced close to consumption, although pipeline gas also varies widely with distance and conditions [8].

That lifecycle premium is not added to the EU ETS surrender obligation for a stationary gas user. If an EU power plant burns grid gas that was physically sourced from LNG, its ETS liability is driven by the direct combustion emissions of the gas it used. Foreign upstream methane leakage, liquefaction emissions, and the power used by an export terminal are not attributed to the power plant's EU ETS account.

The EU policy system does address those lifecycle issues, but mostly through other instruments. The Commission's methane page says the EU Methane Regulation entered into force on 4 August 2024, aims to reduce methane emissions both in the EU and in global supply chains, and phases in importer obligations for crude oil, natural gas, and coal [6]. Importers must provide origin, route, and MRV information from 2025; demonstrate MRV-equivalence or OGMP 2.0 Level 5 plus verification from 2027 for relevant contracts; report methane intensity from 2028; and meet a methane-intensity requirement from 2030 [6].

### Finding 3: Maritime is the important ETS-related exception

Maritime transport is different because the EU ETS has been extended to ships, and LNG can be a ship fuel with methane slip. EMSA states that CO2 emissions from ships of at least 5,000 gross tonnage calling at or departing from EEA ports entered EU ETS from 1 January 2024, and that methane and nitrous oxide are incorporated from 1 January 2026 on a CO2-equivalent basis [7].

Regulation (EU) 2015/757, as amended, requires ship companies to determine greenhouse gas emissions by methods in Annex I [9]. The consolidated text states that companies determine each greenhouse gas separately and total them as CO2-equivalents, and it includes fuel consumed for maritime activities in the scope of Directive 2003/87/EC [9]. Its Annex language says that, for fuels such as LNG with fugitive and slipped emissions, the lost fuel percentage is expressed as a share of fuel used by the specific emission source [10].

This is still not the same as full well-to-burner accounting for LNG used by a land-based power plant. It is tank-to-wake ship emissions, expanded to include methane slip from LNG engines. FuelEU Maritime is the separate regulation that explicitly uses well-to-wake accounting for maritime fuel intensity, including well-to-tank emissions, tank-to-wake CO2 and non-CO2 emissions, and slipped gaseous fuel [11].

## Synthesis

For EU ETS installations and ETS2, there is no generic CO2 accounting uplift for LNG versus piped gas. The EU ETS accounts the emissions from combustion within the regulated boundary, using fuel amount and emission factors. The EU does recognise LNG and gas supply-chain methane/lifecycle issues, but those are handled through the EU Methane Regulation, FuelEU Maritime, and maritime MRV/ETS rules rather than by changing the ordinary ETS emission factor because gas arrived as LNG.

The practical implication is that "LNG has a higher lifecycle footprint" and "LNG creates a higher EU ETS surrender obligation for the burner" are different claims. The first can be true depending on route, leakage, power source, and distance. The second is generally false for stationary ETS and ETS2 unless the fuel composition or approved emission factor itself differs under the monitoring methodology.

## Recommendations

For ETS compliance analysis, model LNG-derived and pipeline-derived gas with the same combustion boundary unless the monitoring plan uses distinct fuel-specific NCV, carbon content, or emission factors. Treat upstream methane, liquefaction, shipping, and regasification as lifecycle or Scope 3 variables rather than EU ETS installation variables.

For policy or procurement analysis, separately track the EU Methane Regulation obligations and any supplier methane-intensity data once available. For maritime, do not reuse the stationary-installation conclusion: EU ETS maritime includes methane and nitrous oxide from 2026, and LNG engine methane slip can materially change ship-level CO2e.

## Limitations

This memo does not quantify supplier-by-supplier lifecycle differences, because those depend heavily on upstream basin, methane leakage, liquefaction power source, shipping distance, regasification energy, and whether the comparison uses 20-year or 100-year methane GWP. It also does not address voluntary corporate Scope 3 accounting, which may treat LNG and pipeline gas differently even when EU ETS does not.
